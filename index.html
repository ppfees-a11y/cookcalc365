Cooking Conversion Calculators — Cups, Grams, Oven Temperatures & Recipe Scaling

Use practical cooking conversions with clearly stated measurement standards. Volume and mass are different quantities: a cup of flour and a cup of honey occupy the same space but do not weigh the same.

US customary volumes are used unless otherwise stated. Ingredient weights are reference estimates; use a scale and follow the recipe author’s measurements when precision matters.

1. Volume converter

Convert US cups, tablespoons, teaspoons, fluid ounces, pints and milliliters. Choose the source and destination units.

Volume-to-volume conversion does not depend on the ingredient.

2. Ingredient cups ↔ grams

Choose an ingredient before converting between volume and weight. Packing and brand differences affect results.

3. Oven temperature

Convert Fahrenheit and Celsius using the standard mathematical formula. Oven calibration and fan settings vary.

Example: 350°F ≈ 176.7°C, commonly rounded to 175°C or 180°C for recipe settings.

4. Recipe servings scaler

Scale one ingredient amount when changing a recipe's number of servings. Seasonings and baking times may need adjustment.

The output uses the same unit as your original ingredient amount.

5. Weight converter

Convert grams, kilograms, ounces and pounds without confusing fluid ounces with weight ounces.

6. Butter converter

US butter sticks are a packaging convention: one standard stick is ½ US cup, 8 tablespoons or approximately 113.4 g.

Why kitchen conversions sometimes disagree

A recipe written for an American audience may use US cups, while a recipe from another country may use metric cups. Although both are called cups, their capacities differ. Even within one country, flour can settle in a container, and scooping it can compress the flour. The calculator cannot detect these conditions, so it displays ingredient-specific reference weights rather than pretending that every cup has a universal gram value.

For a dough or delicate cake, weigh the ingredients when possible. For soups and sauces, volume approximations are often sufficient. If a recipe supplies its own ingredient weights, those values take priority over any general chart.

7. Whole-recipe serving scaler

Paste an entire ingredient list, one ingredient per line, with a number first (for example, 1 1/2 cups flour). Fractions and mixed numbers are supported. Unnumbered directions remain unchanged.

Ingredient quantities scale mathematically. Eggs, spices, salt, yeast, pan depth and cooking times may require judgment; this tool does not automatically alter cooking time.


8. Baking pan area calculator
Compare two round or rectangular pans. If batter depth stays the same, pan area ratio estimates how much batter to prepare; it does not calculate baking time.
Example: 8-inch round to 9-inch round = 81/64 ≈ 1.266 times the batter for equal depth. Do not assume bake time changes by the same factor.


9. Recipe cost per serving
Estimate ingredient spending per portion; useful for meal planning and comparing batch sizes. This is not a grocery-price lookup.


What these results mean
Scaling: The serving multiplier is target servings divided by original servings. It changes the amounts in a recipe, but not necessarily the heat transfer or cooking time. For larger batches, monitor doneness and food safety separately.
Volume and mass: US cups and tablespoons are volume measures. Grams and ounces by weight measure mass. Converting between them requires an ingredient-specific density or reference weight.
Pan area: Two pans with the same surface area can still cook differently if their materials or batter depth differ. Use the area calculation as a starting point, not a promise of identical bake times.
Practical example: A four-serving recipe with 2 cups of flour scaled to ten servings needs 5 cups of flour mathematically. For baking accuracy, a weight-based recipe is often preferable.
